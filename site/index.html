Onchain identity infrastructure for governments and institutions

Most of an identity system has no reason to sit on a public ledger. Credentials, personal data and the act of presenting them belong on a person's device and between the parties concerned. A small set of functions, however, must be shared, current and neutral: records of control, validity, trust and uniqueness that every participant needs the same view of, and whose availability must not depend on the organisation that created them.

This page sets out what those functions are, how they fit together, and why they need not be adopted all at once.

Note on scope. Provided for general educational and informational purposes. It is not legal, regulatory, financial or other professional advice. The implementation described is an unaudited proof of concept intended to support evaluation, not production deployment.

02 — The dividing line

What is recorded, and what never is

One question decides placement: is this information only useful if everyone holds the same view of it, does it change over time, and must it remain available even if the organisation that created it does not? Records of control, validity, trust and uniqueness meet that test. Credential data does not, and is deliberately excluded.

Recorded on the ledger

Shared coordination records

  • Which key controls an identifier right now
  • Who vouches for whom, and for what
  • Whether a credential is still valid
  • Whether someone has already acted in a given context
  • What a verifier publicly commits to requesting
  • Payment between issuers and verifiers

None of this is personal data. No entry means anything about an identifiable person on its own.

Never recorded

Anything about a person

  • Credential contents and attributes
  • Names, identifiers, document numbers
  • Biometrics, or anything derived from them
  • Records of who presented what, and to whom
  • Anything that can be linked back to an individual

A public ledger is permanent. Personal data placed on one cannot be withdrawn, corrected or erased — a compliance failure and an ethical one.

05 — The person

What changes for the individual

Institutional benefit is only half the case. The architecture is only worth adopting if it also improves the position of the person whose identity is being handled — which, in practice, means changing who holds their data and who decides what is disclosed.

Control

The person holds the key, not an account

There is no provider who can suspend the identity, and no database of citizens that can be breached in one action. Guardian recovery means losing a device does not mean losing the identity, and does not require a custodian to hold it on the person's behalf.

Disclosure

Answering the question actually asked

Most checks need one fact — old enough, resident here, licensed for this — not a whole document. Proving the single fact is both better for the person and less burdensome for the organisation, which no longer holds data it did not want and must now protect.

Accountability

The asymmetry runs both ways

The party collecting data becomes as identifiable as the person disclosing it, and publishes what it will request and retain. A wallet can then refuse a request that exceeds a published policy, before any data moves rather than after.

Where the honest limits of this are

Self-sovereignty is a property of the whole deployment, not of any contract. Three caveats are worth stating to an audience deciding policy:

Key management is a real burden. Guardian recovery removes the custodian but places a genuine responsibility on the person. For populations who cannot reasonably carry that, a custodial option will be needed, and offering it re-creates the intermediary for exactly the people with the least power to challenge it.

Proving a fact still requires holding a credential. Whoever issues it retains the power to refuse or withdraw it. This architecture governs what happens after issuance; it does not make issuance fair.

Voluntariness is a policy matter. If a service is only obtainable by presenting a credential, selective disclosure improves the terms but does not make the disclosure optional. That remains a question for law rather than architecture.

06 — Composition

How the parts work together

A worked example. A programme distributes an entitlement once per person rather than once per account, restricted to holders of a credential from an institution its chosen authority accredits, without the programme learning who claimed.

  1. Context binding. The proof is bound to this programme, so the same person's activity elsewhere cannot be linked to it.
  2. Recipient binding. The proof commits to the recipient, so one intercepted in transit is useless to anyone else.
  3. Live policy check. The issuer must hold a current accreditation. Withdraw it and claims stop in the next block, mid-programme.
  4. Proof and uniqueness. The check runs on the ledger, validity is confirmed against a recent record, and a one-time value is consumed so no second claim is possible.

What that amounts to

Roughly forty lines of application code. No list of eligible people to administer, no vendor integration, no verification service, and no personal data anywhere in the system.

The programme did not build an identity system. It inherited one — and each registry added later increases what every programme already running can do, without any of them being changed.

Why this is difficult any other way
Under bilateral integration, each pair of organisations negotiates its own connection, and a new participant must repeat the exercise with everyone already present — so cost grows with the square of participants, and the last to join pays the most. Shared records invert this: each addition makes the arrangement more useful to everyone already in it. That property, rather than any individual mechanism, is the reason to use public infrastructure instead of a consortium platform.

09 — Procurement

What shared public infrastructure provides

These are procurement properties rather than positions of principle. Each answers a question that arises in institutional due diligence and has no good answer in a hosted arrangement.

Continuity

It outlives its operator

Evaluations ask what becomes of issued credentials if a supplier is acquired or leaves the market. The records keep resolving after any single participant ceases to exist, because none of them hosts it.

Neutrality

No party controls access

Registration is open and endorsement is recorded separately, so no operator can exclude a competitor, a jurisdiction or an institution. Each relying party chooses the authorities it recognises.

Simultaneity

Withdrawal is not a distribution problem

Withdrawing trust reaches every relying party in the same block. Published lists, revocation files and status endpoints all carry an interval during which stale information stays in circulation.

Exit

Low cost of leaving

The implementation is open source and forkable, the records are public, and the interfaces are standard. Adopting it creates no position that cannot be left without a redevelopment programme.

Verifiability

The critical checks are public

The logic the system depends on runs where anyone can inspect it and no one can quietly amend it, rather than inside a supplier's system, under contract, evidenced by an assurance report.

Shared cost

Built once, not once per institution

Institutions currently each run a trust list, a revocation service and a set of verifier integrations. This is the same function, built once, with the cost spread across those who benefit.
